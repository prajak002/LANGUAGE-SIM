What form cannot tell: inherited and learned labels in 17 languages

Supplementary results, figures and code for a study of what a model learns from a collaborative dictionary's labels for inherited words and learned borrowings. Every number on this page is read from the result files of the study.

Overview

Many languages have two layers of words from one ancestor: words inherited over centuries, and words borrowed back later from its written record. The dictionary studied here labels tens of thousands of words this way. Models can predict those labels from spelling. This study asks what such a model has learned.

0.86 to 0.96macro-F1 of a minimal form-based model, seven languages descended from Sanskrit
10% and 19%right on the items where label and resemblance disagree (Sanskrit family, Romance)
84%of hard inherited words have an intermediate form that is itself almost the Sanskrit form
3 modelslinear, character Transformer, pretrained character model: none gains from the intermediate form

Problem and objective

The route of a word is a claim about a path. An inherited word passed through an intermediate stage and carries its changes. A learned word did not. A classifier usually sees only the two ends of the path: the modern word and the ancestor form. The study measures how far resemblance to the ancestor explains the labels, where it fails, and whether the intermediate stage, a dictionary or other information decides the cases where it fails.

The target is the dictionary's label, which is not validated. A score measures agreement with that label, not historical accuracy.

Data and experimental setup

  • Pairs: 17 modern languages with an ancestor from which they have both inherited words and learned borrowings (Sanskrit 8, Latin 7, Ancient Greek 1, Old Armenian 1).
  • Sample: up to 400 pages per category in keyed-hash order, 13,128 distinct pages, revision identifiers stored.
  • Items: a sense that states exactly one route from the ancestor and gives an ancestor form: 12,778 items in 16 languages.
  • Models: B1, logistic regression on the normalised edit distance. B2, character 1 to 3-grams of the word. B3, both plus the same n-grams of the ancestor form. Nothing tuned.
  • Evaluation: macro-F1 for inherited against learned, stratified 5-fold cross-validation, out-of-fold predictions kept for every item.
  • Hard items: dissociated items are inherited and within 0.15 of the ancestor form, or learned and at least 0.5 from it. Congruent items are the reverse. The split is defined by a feature of the model, so it is a diagnostic and not an independent test set.

Outside checks

  • A comparative dictionary lists a matching modern form for 57 of 125 near-Sanskrit words labelled inherited and for 100 of 1,001 labelled learned. The automatic matcher agreed with hand records in 31 of 38 rows.
  • For 92 of 110 near-Sanskrit words labelled inherited, the Pali or Prakrit form is itself within 0.15 of the Sanskrit form.
  • Two open expert sources for basic vocabulary judge 48 and 6 of the hard items and agree with the label on 45 and 6. About 95% of inherited-category words in these sources are non-loans whatever their form, so this check has little power.

Reproducibility

  • Environment: Python with numpy 2.5.4, scipy 1.18.1 and scikit-learn 1.9.1 for everything except the two neural models.
  • Seed: 20261011 for folds and models. The Transformer uses 5 seeds and the pretrained model 3.
  • Plans: each analysis followed a plan fixed before its data were read, kept with a SHA-256 hash, unless marked as added later.
  • Runs from stored pages: the audit, the baseline, the hard-item split, the threshold recount, the meaning and weight conditions and the label history can be rerun from the stored page texts.
  • Not reproducible from released files: analyses that read the comparative dictionary use a machine-readable copy that carries no licence. It was used for analysis only and nothing from it is released. The per-item predictions of the pretrained model are not available; its figures come from the printed summary of the run.

Limits

  • No label is validated. Learned labels in particular have no outside check.
  • The hard split is defined by a model feature, so the low score on it is partly by construction.
  • Comparisons are between spellings, not transcriptions.
  • Most learned labels of the Romance history sample were set by one automated account in a single month. The rule it applied is not known.
  • The expert sources cover basic vocabulary and few of the hard items, most of them outside the Sanskrit family.
  • The dictionary lookups of the 48-lemma study were made by a language model under fixed rules, and a person checked 23 of the 134 source records.
